Sermon Study Guide — Revised

He Never Fails

Bishop Henry Emmanuel · Calvary Hephzibah, Sunday 30 August 2026

Three short sessions, one for each of this week and the two after. Each one starts by reading a passage yourself, before any explanation — the aim is to help you read Scripture, not just receive a summary of what was preached. Based on Sunday's message, but not a transcript of it. Take one question you're less sure of, and leave it unresolved if you need to — that's a normal part of this, not a failure at it.

1. When You Feel Afraid

Exodus 14:10–15

Read it first

Before anything else, read Exodus 14:10–15 yourself. Israel is caught between the sea and Pharaoh's army, with no way out that they can see.

Look at the passage

  • What happens in this passage? What do the people do, and what does Moses say back to them?
  • What does this tell us about God — not about the people's feelings, but about what God says He will do?
Worth knowing: "the fear of the LORD," which comes up later, doesn't mean being afraid of God the way Israel is afraid of Pharaoh's army here. It means taking God seriously — treating Him as real and worth honouring, the way you'd take a person seriously rather than dismiss them.

Talk it through

  • Moses tells a genuinely afraid people to "stand still." What's the difference between standing still in trust and standing still because you feel stuck?
  • What might this mean for something you're facing right now where you can't yet see a way through?
  • What are you still left with after reading this — any part that doesn't sit easily, or that you'd want to ask about?

Any of these can be passed on. Silence is a fine answer too.

This week

Name one concern privately — out loud or written down — and bring it to God in prayer once this week. Not a formula, just an honest naming of it.

Leading this session

  • Timing: reading + observe questions ~10 min, discussion ~15 min, application + prayer ~5 min.
  • Welcoming someone new to the Bible: the two "look at the passage" questions are designed so anyone can answer them by reading, not by prior knowledge — lean on those first if the group includes newcomers.
  • If one person is dominating: "let's hear a couple of other people on that one" works better than cutting someone off directly.
  • If someone raises unanswered prayer: don't rush to resolve it or match it to Israel's story too neatly. Acknowledge it plainly ("thank you for saying that") before moving on — you don't need to fix it in the room.

2. Remembering God's Faithfulness

Exodus 14:30–15:2

Read it first

Read Exodus 14:30–15:2. One chapter after the fear of session one, they're singing.

Look at the passage

  • What happened between 14:13 (last session) and here? What actually changed?
  • What does the song in 15:1–2 say about who God is, not just what He did?

Talk it through

  • The song comes after deliverance, not before it. Is there something in your own life you could look back on and genuinely sing about, even briefly?
  • What might this mean for how you hold a situation that hasn't resolved yet — is there a way to remember His past faithfulness without pretending the present is already settled?
  • What are you still left with?

Any of these can be passed on.

This week

Write down one thing you still thank God for from something He's already brought you through. Sharing it with the group is optional.

Leading this session

  • Timing: as session 1.
  • The first observe question deliberately sends people back to compare two passages — give the group a minute of quiet to actually do that rather than answering from memory.
  • Watch for the gap between "God was faithful then" and "so God owes me the same outcome now" — the second isn't what this passage says. If it comes up, it's worth naming gently rather than letting it stand unchallenged.

3. Praying While You Wait

Philippians 4:4–7

Read it first

Read Philippians 4:4–7. Sunday's message referenced verse 4 — "rejoice always" — written by Paul from prison. This session reads the fuller passage around it.

Philippians 4:6–7 (ESV)
"Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus."

Look at the passage

  • What does Paul actually tell the Philippians to do with their anxiety?
  • What does he say the result is — and what does he not promise?
Worth knowing: Paul isn't promising the Philippians their specific requests will be granted exactly as asked. The peace he describes guards the heart during the asking, not as a reward once the outcome is known.

Talk it through

  • Paul pairs "requests" with "thanksgiving" in the same breath. What might it look like to bring an honest request to God and genuine thanks, without needing to already know how it resolves?
  • What might this mean for something you're currently anxious about?
  • What are you still left with?

Any of these can be passed on.

This week

Bring one honest request and one genuine thanks to God in the same prayer, once this week.

Leading this session

  • Timing: as session 1.
  • This is the session most likely to surface real, ongoing pain (financial strain, health, relationships). Don't ask people to name the specific request aloud unless they offer to — "one honest request" in the application is meant to stay private by default.
  • If someone asks "so if I have enough faith, will I get what I'm asking for?" — the honest answer from this passage is no, that's not what it promises. It's fine to say that plainly rather than reach for reassurance the text doesn't give.

Pray this, or pray your own version

Lord, teach me to bring You what I'm carrying honestly, with thanks alongside it, even where I can't yet see how this resolves. Guard my heart while I wait. You have not failed to keep Your word before. Amen.
A note on what changed from the first draft of this guide: an earlier version made a factual error about Paul's imprisonment, stated a number about the Cyrus prophecy that isn't supported by the sermon transcript, and used framing that turned a promise of presence and peace into an implied guarantee of a specific outcome. All three are corrected here — the Cyrus material has been set aside from this guide entirely until it can be separately verified.
