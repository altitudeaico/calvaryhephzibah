Guitar
vs Keys.

A melodic pattern is just the little tune or riff a player keeps repeating — a catchphrase in music. Guitar and keys both love to play them, and they both live in the same part of the sound. So when they both play at once, it turns to mush. This week: how the two of you share.

00 · Where We Left Off

Two Players,
One Spot.

In Vol 02 we said everyone has a spot — high, middle, or low — and if we all crowd the middle it turns to mud. We said: listen first, then find the gap nobody is filling.

Well, here’s the truth — the two players most likely to crowd the exact same spot are the guitar and the keys. They both sit in the middle. They both can play chords. They both can play little tunes on top. They’re basically twins fighting over one chair.

So this week we zoom right in on those two. If guitar and keys learn to share, half the mud in our sound clears up on its own.

01 · The Big Idea

Two Voices,
One Story.

Picture two people telling you the same story at the same time. Even if both are lovely, you can’t follow either one — it’s just noise. But if one talks while the other nods, then they swap, you hear the whole thing. Guitar and keys are those two people.

A melodic pattern is a voice. When guitar and keys both start "talking" — both playing busy little lines — the ear can’t hold both. It gives up and hears a blur. The fix isn’t to play better. It’s to take turns, and to sound different enough that we can tell you apart.

Paul actually said this about instruments, two thousand years ago:

1 Corinthians 14 · 7

"Even things without life, giving a voice, whether pipe or harp, if they didn’t give a distinction in the sounds, how would it be known what is piped or harped?"

Instruments must sound distinct · WEB translation

Read that again — he’s talking about a pipe and a harp. His point: if the notes don’t come out distinct — clear and separate — nobody can tell what’s being played. That’s guitar and keys in one verse. Our job is to make sure each line comes out clear, not smeared into the other.

02 · Why It Clashes

The Same
Sandbox.

Guitar and keys clash for one simple reason: they’re playing in the same sandbox. Same middle range, same kind of job, same instinct to fill every gap. Two kids, one bucket — someone’s getting sand in the face.

Think about what each one can do. Keys can play chords, and a tune on top, and low notes underneath. Guitar can play chords, and a riff, and shiny high bits. Loads of overlap. That’s the problem — not that either one is wrong, but that they keep reaching for the same job at the same time.

The Bible has a picture for this too — a body where every part tries to be the same part:

1 Corinthians 12 · 17

"If the whole body were an eye, where would the hearing be? If the whole were hearing, where would the smelling be?"

If everyone’s the same, something’s missing · WEB translation

If guitar and keys both play the same busy line, it’s like a body that’s all eyes — something important is missing. Nobody’s holding the cushion. Nobody’s leaving space. So the rule is simple: don’t both be the eye. One of you does a different job.

03 · The Rule

One Holds.
One Moves.

Here’s the whole lesson in four words. At any moment, one of you holds — a soft cushion of chords underneath — and the other moves — the little tune on top. Then you swap. Never both moving at once.

Three easy ways to stay out of each other’s way:

→ Different jobs. One holds a pad (long, soft chords — a cushion of sound). The other plays the pattern. Morayo, Eddie — decide before the song who’s holding and who’s moving, and swap by section.

→ Different rooms. If you both must play, get out of the same octave. One goes low and warm, the other high and sparkly. Same idea as high / middle / low from last time.

→ Different rhythm. One plays long notes, the other plays short. If keys hold and guitar plucks, the ear can easily tell you apart — two different shapes, not one blur.

04 · The Heart of It

Leave
a Gap.

Here’s the mindset behind all of it. The gap is not empty — it’s an invitation. When you stop playing for a moment, you’re not being lazy. You’re handing the space to the other player so their line can be heard. Silence from you is a gift to them.

So try playing half as much. Leave holes. Let the other instrument answer. Call and response — you play a line, they reply in your gap — sounds a hundred times better than both of you playing all the time. Two clear lines beat one muddy wall of sound, every time.

1 Corinthians 14 · 33

"For God is not a God of confusion, but of peace."

Clear, not cluttered · WEB translation

That’s the same chapter as the pipe and the harp — Paul cared that worship came out clear, not confusing. A muddy wall of guitar-and-keys fighting is confusion. Two lines taking turns, each with room to breathe, is peace. And peace is what makes space for God to move. That’s the real reason we bother sharing the sound.

"Two clear lines beat one muddy wall."

▸ Carry this onto the platform

05 · So Here’s the Job

Four Things
to Take to Sunday.

Hold These Four.

1. One holds, one moves. One plays the cushion, the other plays the tune. Never both busy at once.

2. Different rooms. If you both play, get out of the same octave — one low, one high.

3. Different rhythm. One long, one short. Two different shapes, so the ear can tell you apart.

4. Leave a gap. The space is where the other player lives. Play half as much.

If you only remember one, remember this: one holds, one moves. Decide who’s who before the song starts, and we already sound twice as clear.

"Two clear lines beat one muddy wall."
