Loud, Soft,
& Space.

There's a big word — dynamics. Here's the easy meaning: when to play loud, when to play soft, and how to give everyone room. That's the whole lesson. It's the thing that makes a song come alive.

00 · Where We Left Off

Last Time
We Got the Shape.

In Vol 01 we agreed the shape our praise should follow: come in strong, pull back on the verses so the words can be heard, then build it back up for the chorus.

That's the map. But a map only helps if you know how to walk it. So this week is the how. The thing that lets you actually do strong-then-soft-then-big has a name, and the name is dynamics.

You already watched the Paul Baloche video I sent. Good. This page is just us turning what he showed into a few simple things we can all do.

01 · The Big Idea

A Song Is
a Hill.

Think of a good song like walking up a hill and back down. It doesn't stay flat. It climbs, reaches the top, comes down, and climbs again. The climbing is the song getting bigger. The coming down is the song getting softer.

Every film you love does this. Quiet bits, then big bits, then quiet again — that's what keeps you watching. Music is exactly the same. If a song is loud the whole way through, your ears stop noticing. If it's soft the whole way through, nothing ever lifts. The change is the magic.

And this isn't just a music idea. God built the whole world to move in seasons — moments that are meant to be different from each other:

Ecclesiastes 3 · 1, 4, 7

"For everything there is a season… a time to weep, and a time to laugh… a time to keep silence, and a time to speak."

A time for everything · WEB translation

That's dynamics, straight out of the Bible. There is a time to be quiet and a time to be loud. The skill is knowing which time you're in and playing for that moment — not blasting through the quiet bit, and not hiding through the big bit. Here's the hill for most of our songs:

01
Intro
▸ Medium → Strong
Set the scene. Come in with enough so the room knows something good is starting. Don't be shy, but you don't have to give everything away yet.
02
Verse
▸ Soft — this is the quiet bit
Pull right back. This is where the words live. The singer carries it, and we get out of the way. Less playing, not more. Let it breathe.
03
Chorus
▸ Loud — top of the hill
Now everyone's in. Full and lifted. This is the top of the hill. Then we come back down for the next verse and climb again.

Soft, then loud, then soft, then loud. The pattern is what makes praise feel like praise.

Watch the bass player. At the start he plays high and simple — just a few long notes. As the chorus gets close, he plays more notes and goes lower. He didn't get louder by banging harder. He got bigger by doing more and going lower. That's the trick. Hold that thought — it's the next thing.

03 · The Golden Rule

Loud Is Not
the Same as Fast.

Here's the mistake every band makes. When a song gets exciting, we get excited — and we speed up. Then it falls apart, because everyone is rushing and nobody is together.

So remember this one for life: the beat stays the same the whole way through. The speed is like a fence post stuck in the ground — straight and still. It does not move. The loud-and-soft is the only thing going up and down.

So how do you get bigger without getting faster? Two ways, the same as the bass player in the video:

→ Play more — busier, more notes, instead of simple.
→ Add the low end — bass and the low keys coming in for the chorus.

Baloche's tip: try it at home. Keep your left hand tapping a slow, steady beat that never changes. Now let your right hand get busier and busier on top. The left hand is the beat. The right hand is the energy. Both at once.

"Get bigger by playing more — never by playing faster."

▸ The one to remember · for Calvary Worship

04 · Make Room

Everyone Has
a Spot.

Picture a family photo. If everyone crowds into the exact same place, you can't see anyone — it's a mess. But if each person stands in their own spot, you can see the whole family. Instruments are the same.

There are high sounds, middle sounds, and low sounds. If we all play in the middle, it turns to mud — a thick, muddy noise where you can't hear anyone properly. So we spread out. Each of us takes our own spot:

High
Electric guitar, top of the keys, high voices. The sparkly bits that sit on top — little hooks and shiny lines.
Middle
Acoustic guitar, the main keys, most voices. The warm middle where most of the song lives.
Low
Bass and the low keys, kick drum. The deep floor that holds everything up. Big and important — but only one or two of us live down here.

If two of you are fighting for the same spot, one of you should move — go higher, or go lower, or play less. Listen first, then find the gap nobody is filling. That's what "thinking like an arranger" means: not just playing your part, but making sure the whole picture has room in it.

2 Chronicles 5 · 13–14

"The trumpeters and singers were as one, to make one sound to be heard… then the house was filled with a cloud… for the glory of the LORD filled God's house."

When they played as one · WEB translation

Look at what happened there. When all the players and singers stopped being separate and became one sound — that's the moment God's presence filled the room. That's the whole reason we bother with any of this. We don't learn dynamics to sound clever. We learn it so we can play as one, and make room for God to move.

05 · The Hard One

Sometimes the
Best Note Is None.

This is the hardest one to learn, so hear me out. You do not have to play all the time. Holding back is not being lazy — it's a skill, and it's one of the kindest things you can do for the team.

When the singer is singing the verse, that's their moment. If we all pile in, the words get buried and the singer is left shouting over us. So we go quiet. We make room. Paul put it like this: "in honour preferring one another" (Romans 12:10) — letting the other person be heard first. Going quiet for the singer is how a musician honours.

And save the big stuff. Don't use up all your loud, all your low end, all your busy playing in the first ten seconds. If you spend it early, you've got nothing left for the chorus. Keep something in the tank so the top of the hill actually feels like the top.

Psalm 33 · 3

"Sing to him a new song. Play skilfully with a shout of joy!"

Play skilfully · WEB translation

"Play skilfully" doesn't mean play the most notes. It means play the right notes, in the right spot, at the right size. Loud when it should be loud. Soft when it should be soft. Quiet when it's someone else's turn. That's skill. That's worship done well.

And skill has always mattered to God. When King David set up the worship, the man he put in charge of the music — Kenaniah — was given the job because he was skilful at it (1 Chronicles 15:22). So when you practise your part this week, that's not showing off. That's you taking your offering seriously.

"Quiet is not weak. Quiet is where the words live."

▸ Carry this onto the platform

06 · So Here's the Job

Five Things
to Take to Sunday.

Hold These Five.

1. A song is a hill. Soft, then loud, then soft again. Never flat.

2. The beat never speeds up. Get bigger by playing more, not faster.

3. Everyone has a spot. High, middle, or low. Don't all crowd the middle.

4. Make room for the singer. Verses are quiet. The words come first.

5. Keep some in the tank. Save the big stuff for the top of the hill.

If you only remember one, remember number two. More, not faster. Get that one right and we already sound twice as good.

"Quiet is not weak. Quiet is where the words live."

Let's bring loud, soft, and space to everything we play. See you on the call.
